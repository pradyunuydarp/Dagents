GRAILS keeps two things that Dagents gives up. A learned or fuzzy trust model can
weigh attributes that a fixed formula cannot, and a language model can read new
regulations without a person writing a classification. Dagents trades these for
plans that are reproducible and reviewable, on the view that an audit needs to
compute the same plan again from the same request.

\subsection{Who needs this design}

The design fits a setting with four properties: several organisations hold data
they may not pool; a study needs both statistics and trained models from that
data; the rules about what may leave differ by field, requester and purpose; and
an auditor must be able to see, later, why each thing that left was allowed to
leave. Hospital consortia and research networks have all four. So does a
software service whose tenants agree to a shared model but not to shared rows,
and a public data portal whose custodians keep their own stores, which is
GRAILS's setting extended to several custodians.

Other settings need less. Data that already sits in one warehouse can use the
warehouse's masking and aggregation policies (F2). A release that must carry a
differential privacy guarantee needs a DP library and an accountant (F3),
which Dagents does not provide. A consortium that governs participation and
release through agreements and committees, and needs only a learning runtime,
can use FLARE or Flower directly (F4).

\subsection{Why not assemble existing parts}

A team could assemble most of this from mature parts: Cedar or OPA for
participation, FLARE's site filters for what leaves, a DP library for noise, and
a model registry with a reviewer for release. Each part is stronger than its
counterpart in Dagents. What the team would still write is the glue that the
gaps in \cref{sec:synthesis} describe: a per-field transformation chosen from
trust, purpose and granularity; the same rule applied at four boundaries; the
rules that turn a candidate into a release; a deny default at every boundary;
and records that name the logic that decided. Dagents packages that glue, and
its interfaces let the mature parts sit behind it: a learning runtime behind
\code{FederationEngine}, and a DP mechanism as a strategy the Guard applies.
Against this, Dagents is an alpha release with one worked study. Where a team
has the mature parts and the staff to integrate them, assembling them may be the
better choice.

\subsection{Why a typed planner instead of a policy language}

Policy languages such as Rego and Cedar decide authorisation, and Cedar's
analysis is stronger than anything Dagents offers. Dagents's planners return
structured results: a strategy and a reason for each field, obligations, round
plans with exclusion reasons, lifecycle status changes, and deployment
manifests. One typed language for all of these gives one exhaustiveness check
and one test loop. The cost is that a reviewer must read OCaml, and that the
planner has no formal semantics. Compiling the strategy table to an analysable
form, or checking its properties with an SMT solver, is future work.
